The product in one paragraph

The catalog lets people in the organisation find datasets and understand them (contract, lineage, quality, consumers, access). Most people are consumers who search and read. Admins also register new datasets. The product is healthy when people get in without friction, find a dataset quickly, come back, and when admins can keep the catalog full.

North Star metric

Weekly users who view at least one dataset. It counts people who got what the product exists to give them. Login success and search are inputs to it. Registration feeds it from the supply side, because a catalog with few datasets has little to view.

In Amplitude: Event Segmentation, event Dataset Viewed, measured as unique users, weekly.

How to read it

  • The funnel shows where people drop out between seeing the login screen and being signed in. Each bar is the number of distinct users who reached that step. The time on each step is in the Amplitude chart's tooltip.
  • The failures chart separates invalid_credentials (a typo or a forgotten password) from account_locked (three failures in a row). Locks are the expensive ones: they end in a support ticket.
What the data says. Almost everyone gets in, and gets in fast. Failures rise with traffic, so read them as a rate, not a count. 22 October stands out: 6 failed attempts (5 wrong credentials, 1 lockout) against only 9 users signing in that day. That is the pattern of a flaky login window or a bad rollout, not ordinary typos.
What I would do. Add an alert on failure rate per day, not on count. Ask why a lockout happens after only three tries, and check whether a "forgot password" link is visible on the failed-login screen. Track lockouts as a support-cost KPI.
Limit of this chart. The funnel counts a user once, even if they had several login sessions in the window. A user who gave up in one session and succeeded in another counts as converted, so 99.4% hides session-level abandonment. For per-session health, use a session-based funnel in the Amplitude UI.

How to read it

  • Source mix tells you which doors people use. Catalog is browsing, search is typing a query, suggestion is clicking a suggested dataset, home is a shortcut from the start page. A healthy product moves people from slow doors (browsing) to fast ones (search, suggestions).
  • Search to open measures whether a search leads anywhere. A drop means results are poor, not that people stopped searching.
What the data says. Suggestions were almost unused before late October: 21 of 164 views, or 13%. After they launched they carry 306 of 892 views, or 34%, and they overtake typed search. The launch worked as a feature: people prefer to click a suggestion than to type. Search is efficient too: 98% of searchers open a dataset, and they do so in seven seconds at the median.
What I would do. Treat the suggestion share as the success metric for the search project and set a target (for example 40%). Look at which dataset ids dominate suggestion clicks, to see whether the list is too narrow. Next chart to build: Dataset Tab Viewed by tab, to see which parts of a dataset page (contract, lineage, quality) people actually read.
Gap in the tracking plan. The 98% only counts people who opened something. A search that returns nothing and is abandoned is not captured as such, because Search Performed carries only query_length and query_words. Add a results_count property to measure zero-result searches, usually the best signal of what is missing from a catalog.

What the data says. The largest relative loss is between step 1 and step 2 (3 of 13 admins), and that is also the slowest step: a median of seven minutes. Once admins finish step 2, nine in ten complete. So the problem is the long details form, not the final submit.
What I would do. Split step 2 or make some fields optional; pre-fill columns from the table in step 1; keep the draft (there is already a resumed_draft property, so measure how many come back). Watch Dataset Registration Abandoned by last_step to confirm where people leave.
Sample size. Fifteen admins is too few for a firm conclusion: one admin is 7 percentage points. Use it to decide where to look, not to set a target.

Retention, start and return event both Dataset Viewed. Each cell is the share of that cohort who viewed a dataset again that week. Overall retained: week 1 is 72 of 175 users.

How to read it

  • The sign-ins chart shows growth and who it comes from. The jump in the third week of the window is the rollout, when new teams were onboarded: daily sign-ins rise from single digits to around 50 to 60.
  • The retention table groups people by the week they first viewed a dataset and follows them. Read each row left to right, and compare rows top to bottom to see whether newer cohorts retain better or worse.
What the data says. Older cohorts are sticky: the first cohort returned at 93% in week 1. The cohort sizes explain the fall-off in newer rows: the rollout brought 115 people in each of the last two weeks, and a rollout cohort behaves differently from early adopters, who chose the tool. The 43% for the 2 November cohort is also incomplete, because its week 1 is the week still in progress.
What I would do. Wait for the 2 November cohort to finish before judging it. Then compare early adopters against rollout users: if rollout retention settles well below, build onboarding for people who did not choose the tool (a first-dataset tour, saved searches, team-level starter lists). Keep admins as a separate segment: they are 15% of users but all of the supply.

Caveats and next steps

  • This is synthetic data. It was generated with built-in stories (a login failure window, a suggestions launch, a drop at registration step 2, a rollout spike) so every chart has something to explain. Patterns here show the dashboard works, not how real users behave.
  • Two analyses are not saved as Amplitude charts because the starter plan limits saved charts: weekly retention and sign-ins by role. They appear on this page, and as a text block on the dashboard.
  • Funnels count users, not sessions, so they hide repeated attempts.
  • Mock and real data share a project. Keep the is_mock filter on every chart, or move the real site to its own project before launch.
  • Next steps: add results_count to Search Performed; chart tab usage; build cohorts for power users, one-off users and admins; set targets for the four lead KPIs once real data exists.
